How Tokyo Locals Actually Relax: A River Walk and the City's Most Luxurious Bathhouse

Ask a Tokyo local how they recover from this city — the trains, the crowds, the pace — and almost none of them will say a rooftop bar or a shopping street. They'll tell you about the bath. Public bathing is one of Japan's most beloved traditions: a weekly ritual, not a luxury. And 25 minutes from Ikebukuro, at the end of a quiet river trail almost no visitor has ever walked, sits one of the largest and most luxurious bathhouse complexes in the Tokyo area.

This is the half-day I built my Spa Japon tour around, and it's the one guests consistently call the most relaxing thing they did in Japan.

The river tourists never find

We start at Higashi-Kurume Station, a stop on the Seibu Ikebukuro line that has never appeared on a "top things to do in Tokyo" list — which is exactly the point. From the east exit it's a ten-minute walk to the Kurome River, a clear, quiet waterway that locals adore and guidebooks ignore. The riverside path is flat and easy: about two kilometers of bamboo groves, small neighborhood shrines, and the low hum of everyday Tokyo life. In spring the banks go pink with cherry blossoms; in summer the trail is deep green shade; in autumn everything turns gold.

Halfway along we step into the grounds of Hosenji, a small Buddhist temple wrapped in tall trees and moss-covered stone lanterns. It has been a place of prayer for centuries, and it is completely, reliably empty of tour groups. We stop, breathe, and keep walking.

Spadium Japon — the payoff

Then comes the reason to come all this way: Spadium Japon, one of the biggest and most luxurious public bath complexes in greater Tokyo. Open-air baths looking onto a garden. Carbonated baths that fizz gently against your skin. Jet baths, aroma saunas, and natural hot-spring water that leaves you feeling deeply, chemically calm. Towels and every amenity are provided — you bring nothing but yourself. Afterwards there's a stylish café upstairs for a drink or dessert before a ten-minute shuttle bus drops us back at the station.

If you've never done a Japanese bathhouse before, this is the place to learn. I walk every guest through the etiquette — where to wash, what the unspoken rules are, why bathing here is a social ritual rather than a spa treatment — so that by the time you're in the water, you're not a tourist doing something exotic. You're just another person in the bath, which is the whole point.

The practical details

The tour runs about 2.5 hours door to door from Higashi-Kurume Station, with spa entry, the shuttle bus, and a café drink included. A few honest caveats: the baths are gender-separated, swimwear isn't allowed (that's every Japanese bathhouse, not just this one), and guests with tattoos can't enter the bathing area — Japan is slowly changing on this, but Spadium Japon hasn't yet. The river walk is flat and gentle, so any fitness level is fine.
