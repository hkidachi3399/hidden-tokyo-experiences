Tokyo Autumn Leaves 2026: A Local's West Tokyo Guide (No Nikko Crowds)

Every October, the same question shows up in my inbox: "Should we do Nikko or Kyoto for the leaves?" My honest answer is usually neither — not because they aren't beautiful, but because a shuttle bus queue and three thousand tripods rather ruins the mood a maple forest is supposed to create. I've spent every autumn for the past several years walking the same handful of spots on the Tokyo-Saitama border, and I still haven't run into a tour group.

This isn't a "hidden gems" listicle written from a desk. These are the two places I actually take guests to in November, plus the timing notes I wish someone had given me before I learned them the hard way — mostly by showing up two weeks too early with a very patient client.

Why the western edge of Tokyo wins on timing

Kyoto and Nikko peak on almost the same two-week window, which is exactly why they're unbearable — everyone with the same guidebook arrives at once. The Tokyo-Saitama border area (Niiza, Tokorozawa, Sayama) sits at a slightly cooler elevation and a few days' different microclimate, so the color arrives on its own schedule. It also simply isn't in the guidebooks that send tour buses, which does most of the work for you.

Spot 1: Heirinji's maple valley (this week's pick)

Heirinji, the working Zen monastery in Niiza I've written about before, holds over a hundred varieties of maple in the valley behind its cemetery path. Late November is peak — the temple itself is quiet even then, since most visitors still default to the famous names. I'm featuring this walk this week for exactly that reason: the color is starting to turn at the edges now, and dates for peak-week slots go first. I run a small-group walk here, matcha included, and you can read the fuller story of the temple itself in my earlier piece on Heirinji.

Spot 2: The Sayama Hills, Totoro's real forest

Forty minutes from Ikebukuro, the Sayama Hills turn in a gentler, more scattered way than Heirinji's concentrated maple valley — golden ginkgo and rust-colored oak along the trails that inspired My Neighbor Totoro, with a farmer's tea stop at the end. It reads less like a postcard and more like an actual walk in the woods, which is the point. Full details on that tour here.

When to actually go

If you only remember one date range: aim for the third week of November through the first week of December. Early November is still green at this elevation — I've had guests arrive on November 3rd expecting fireworks and gotten mostly leaves that hadn't made up their mind yet. Weekday mornings beat weekend afternoons everywhere in Japan, but it matters less out here than in the city, since there was rarely a crowd to begin with.

Bring a layer you can take off. West Tokyo autumn mornings run cold enough for a jacket and warm enough by early afternoon that you'll regret wearing it on the walk back.
