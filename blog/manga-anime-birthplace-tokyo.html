Manga Wasn't Born in Akihabara — It Was Born on This Quiet Street

Every week, thousands of visitors ride the Yamanote Line to Akihabara looking for the soul of manga and anime. They find billboards, maid cafés and eight floors of merchandise. What they don't find is the place where any of it actually began — because that place is a sleepy residential street on the other side of the city, twenty minutes from Ikebukuro, where almost nobody goes.

The street is in Minami-Nagasaki, in Toshima Ward, and the building that once stood there was called Tokiwaso. If manga has a birthplace, this is it.

One apartment building, an entire art form

Tokiwaso was nothing special to look at: a two-storey wooden apartment block, four-and-a-half-mat rooms, a shared kitchen, no bath. But in the 1950s its tenant list reads like the founding pantheon of Japanese pop culture. Osamu Tezuka — the "god of manga," creator of Astro Boy — lived here. So did the two young men who would become Fujiko Fujio and go on to create Doraemon. So did Shotaro Ishinomori, who later invented Kamen Rider and Cyborg 009, and Fujio Akatsuka, the king of gag manga.

They were poor, mostly in their early twenties, and they lived stacked on top of each other — trading techniques, deadlines and instant ramen. When one of them fell behind on a manuscript, the others would pick up pens and help. Modern manga's collaborative studio culture, its visual grammar, even its work ethic: an astonishing amount of it traces back to a handful of rooms in this one building.

On my small-group walk through this neighborhood, I take a maximum of three guests through the backstreets the artists actually lived in — museum entry included, no crowds, no flags.

The museum that rebuilt a legend

The original Tokiwaso was demolished in 1982, and for decades the birthplace of manga existed only in memory and in the manga the residents drew about their younger selves. Then, in 2020, Toshima Ward rebuilt it — faithfully, obsessively — as the Tokiwaso Manga Museum, a short walk from the original site.

Climbing the recreated staircase is the moment guests go quiet. The creaking wood, the narrow corridor, the tiny rooms with ink bottles and manuscript pages laid out mid-deadline — it is a time machine to 1955. You begin to understand that the stories that conquered the world were drawn by kids in freezing rooms who could barely afford noodles.

The neighborhood is half the story

What makes this walk special isn't only the museum — it's that Minami-Nagasaki itself hasn't changed much. The shopping street the artists shopped on still runs past the station. The ramen shop where they ordered late-night bowls is still serving. Monuments and manga art are scattered along the streets for those who know where to look, and my job is to be the one who knows where to look.

This is also, quietly, one of the best ways to see everyday Tokyo life. No tour buses come here. You'll walk past greengrocers, primary schools and old kissaten cafés — the ordinary, lovely Tokyo that surrounded the extraordinary people who lived here.

Practical notes

The walk takes about half a day and is flat and easy — comfortable shoes are enough. The museum is closed on Mondays, so plan around that. Photography is allowed in most areas, and yes, the gift shop is dangerously good. Come with the manga or anime you loved as a kid in mind; telling me about it is half the fun, and I'll connect it back to the artists who started it all.
