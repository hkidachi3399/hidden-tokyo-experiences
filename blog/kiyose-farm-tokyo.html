Tokyo's Most Unscripted Morning: A Real Kiyose Farm, a JA Market, and Giant Tempura

Every tour I run has some kind of script — a temple with a known history, a brewery with a set tasting counter, a museum with a fixed exhibit. This one doesn't. I took over a small plot at a community farm in Kiyose this September, and I honestly don't know what's going to be in the ground on the morning you show up. That's not a marketing line. It's the whole point.

Kiyose sits right at Tokyo's northwestern edge, where the 23 wards quietly give way to some of the most productive farmland in the metropolis. Nobody puts it on a first-time itinerary, which is exactly why I built a tour around a Saturday morning that was already happening whether anyone booked it or not.

The farm nobody's staged for you

We meet at Kiyose Station's north exit and take a short local bus (fare's not included — pay in cash on the day, like everyone else does) to the Miyanodai-jutaku stop, then walk five minutes to My Farm Kiyose, a small community garden wedged between rice paddies and a quiet residential block. There, guests join in on whatever the plot actually needs that week: weeding, watering, general upkeep. If there's something ready to pick, we pick it together — but I treat that as a bonus, not a promise. I only started working this plot in September, so some weeks are more generous than others. That uncertainty is the honest part most "farm experience" tours quietly edit out.

Shopping like a local, not for one

Five minutes further on is Mirai Kiyose Shinsenkan, the JA (Japan Agricultural Cooperative) direct-sale market where Kiyose's farmers bring in what they picked that morning. Kiyose is known locally for its carrots, and you'll find them here in every form — fresh, pickled, turned into snacks — sitting next to produce that never makes it as far as central Tokyo. This isn't a market dressed up for visitors; it's where the neighborhood actually buys its vegetables, and you shop it the same way they do.

Standing-room soba and a tempura you'll remember

The morning wraps up another five-minute walk away at Marusu Soba Udon, a tachigui (standing-eating) noodle counter that only opened in spring 2025 and has already become a local fixture. There's no English menu and, on a normal weekday, no other tourists — just the neighborhood's lunch counter, best known for tempura so large the eggplant alone barely fits the bowl. One note if you're planning a Sunday visit: Marusu is closed, so we eat somewhere else near Kiyose Station instead — soba, sushi, and a few other solid options, all within walking distance.

If you'd rather stretch the morning into something more restorative, Option B swaps in a stop at Spa Japon in Higashikurume — one train stop away — for a proper Japanese bath after the fieldwork. Same farm, same market, same tempura, with a soak added at the end.

Who this is actually for

This isn't the tour for someone who wants a checklist of famous sights. It's for the traveler who's already done the temples and the crossing photos and wants to know what an ordinary Saturday in Tokyo's farmland actually looks like — dirt under the fingernails included. Most weekends I'm out at this same plot with or without a booking. Joining the tour just means you're there for it too.
